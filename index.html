Introduction to probability

Probability is a numerical measure of the likelihood that an event will occur. It always takes a value between 0 and 1:

$$ 0 \leq P(A) \leq 1 $$

where \(P(A) = 0\) represents an impossible event and \(P(A) = 1\) represents a certain event.

For outcomes that are equally likely,

$$ P(A) = \frac{\text{Number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of possible outcomes}} $$

Example — rolling a fair die

For a die numbered 1 through 6,

$$ S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\} $$

If \(A\) is the event of obtaining a prime number,

$$ A = \{2, 3, 5\} $$

therefore,

$$ P(A) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5 $$

Basic terminology

Four terms recur throughout the module and are worth fixing precisely before going further.

Random experiment. An experiment whose exact outcome cannot be predicted with certainty. Example: rolling a die.

Outcome. A single possible result of an experiment. Example: obtaining a 4.

Sample space, \(S\). The set of all possible outcomes.

$$ S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\} $$

Event. Any subset of the sample space.

$$ A = \{\text{prime number}\} = \{2, 3, 5\} $$
3

Axioms of probability

Three axioms form the mathematical foundation that everything else in the module is built on. For any event \(A\),

$$ P(A) \geq 0 $$

For the entire sample space,

$$ P(S) = 1 $$

For mutually exclusive events \(A\) and \(B\) — events that cannot occur together —

$$ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) $$
4

Compound probability

Compound probability describes how two or more events combine. There are two rules to keep straight.

The AND rule

For two events occurring together,

$$ P(A \cap B) = P(A) \, P(B \mid A) $$

If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent — knowing one tells you nothing about the other — this simplifies to

$$ P(A \cap B) = P(A) \, P(B) $$

The OR rule

For any two events, not necessarily mutually exclusive,

$$ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) $$

The intersection is subtracted because outcomes in \(A \cap B\) would otherwise be counted twice — once inside \(P(A)\) and once inside \(P(B)\).

5

Conditional probability

Conditional probability is the probability of an event occurring given that another event is already known to have occurred:

$$ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} $$

provided \(P(B) > 0\). The formula restricts attention to the world in which \(B\) is true, then asks what fraction of that restricted world also satisfies \(A\).

Example — die, revisited

Let

$$ A = \{\text{prime}\} = \{2, 3, 5\}, \qquad B = \{\text{odd}\} = \{1, 3, 5\} $$

Then

$$ A \cap B = \{3, 5\} $$

so

$$ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{2/6}{3/6} = \frac{2}{3} $$

Restricting to the three odd outcomes \(\{1,3,5\}\), two of them — 3 and 5 — are also prime, which is exactly what the ratio \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) reports.

Venn diagrams

Venn diagrams give a visual account of how events relate to one another, and they make the OR rule's subtraction of the overlap easy to see at a glance.

The shaded overlap is \(A \cap B\) — counted once in \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\)

These diagrams are especially useful for reasoning about

$$ A \cup B, \qquad A \cap B, \qquad A^{c} $$

and for building intuition about conditional probability before moving into the joint, conditional, and marginal distributions that close out Module 1.

8

Bayes' theorem

Bayes' theorem updates the probability of an event as new evidence becomes available — it inverts a conditional probability you know into one you want:

$$ P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B)\, P(B)}{P(A)} $$

Because it turns "probability of the evidence given the cause" into "probability of the cause given the evidence," it underlies a wide range of inference problems:

  • Medical diagnosis — inferring a condition from a test result
  • Spam filtering — inferring spam from the words in a message
  • Machine learning classification — inferring a class label from features
  • Fault detection — inferring a fault from an observed symptom
9

Random variables

Random variable. A function that assigns a numerical value to each outcome of a random experiment.

Example — tossing a coin twice

Let \(X\) be the number of heads obtained:

$$ X = \{0, 1, 2\} $$

Discrete random variable

Takes a countable set of values.

  • Number of customers arriving at a counter
  • Number of defective items in a batch

Continuous random variable

Can take any value within an interval.

  • Height
  • Temperature
  • Time

Expectation

The expectation of a random variable is its long-run average value. For a discrete variable,

$$ E(X) = \sum x\, P(x) $$

Example — average movies watched

Using the distribution from the previous section,

$$ E(X) = 0(0.1) + 1(0.5) + 2(0.4) = 1.3 $$
12

Variance and standard deviation

Variance measures how spread out a random variable's values are around its mean, \(\mu\):

$$ Var(X) = E\!\left[(X - \mu)^2\right] $$

Standard deviation is simply its square root, expressed in the same units as \(X\):

$$ \sigma = \sqrt{Var(X)} $$
13

Binomial distribution

The binomial distribution applies when an experiment consists of:

  • A fixed number of trials
  • Two possible outcomes per trial
  • A constant probability of success across trials

Examples

Tossing a coin a fixed number of times; a fixed number of pass/fail experiments.

$$ P(X = x) = \binom{n}{x} p^x q^{\,n-x} $$

where

$$ q = 1 - p $$

with mean and variance

$$ \mu = np, \qquad \sigma^2 = npq $$
14

Poisson distribution

The Poisson distribution is used for counting rare events over a fixed interval of time or space.

Examples

Number of calls received per hour; number of accidents per month; number of defects per batch.

$$ P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x}}{x!} $$

with mean and variance both equal to the rate parameter:

$$ \mu = \lambda, \qquad \sigma^2 = \lambda $$
