Axioms and fundamental concepts

Probability measures how likely an event is, expressed as a fraction, decimal or percentage. The sample space \(\Omega\) is the set of all outcomes; an event \(A\subseteq\Omega\) is a set of outcomes.

Kolmogorov axioms. A probability \(P\) satisfies:
  • Non-negativity: \(P(A)\ge 0\) for every event \(A\).
  • Normalisation: \(P(\Omega)=1\).
  • Additivity: for mutually exclusive events \(A_1,A_2,\dots\), \(\;P\!\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right)=\sum_i P(A_i)\).

Useful consequences: \(P(A^c)=1-P(A)\), \(P(\varnothing)=0\), \(0\le P(A)\le 1\), and \(A\subseteq B\Rightarrow P(A)\le P(B)\).

When all outcomes are equally likely (the classical case):

$$P(\text{event})=\frac{\text{number of desired outcomes}}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}}$$

For example, when rolling a fair six-sided die there are six possible outcomes, \(1,2,3,4,5,6\). If we want one particular number there is only one desired outcome, so

$$P=\frac{1}{6}$$

Sample space and outcome

A sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. For example, when a coin is flipped, there are two possible outcomes:

$$S=\{H,T\}$$

where \(H\) represents heads and \(T\) represents tails. An outcome is the result of a single trial or experiment. For example, if a coin is flipped and lands on heads, then Heads (H) is the outcome.

The probability scale

Probability describes the likelihood or chance that an event will occur. It is measured on a scale from 0 to 1.

  • \(P(E)=0\): the event will not happen.
  • \(P(E)=1\): the event is certain to happen.
  • \(0\lt P(E)\lt 1\): the event may or may not happen.

The closer the probability is to 1, the more likely the event is to occur.

a) Theoretical probability

Theoretical probability describes what should happen based on all possible outcomes.

$$P(E)=\frac{\text{Number of desired outcomes}}{\text{Total number of possible outcomes}}$$

For example, when a fair coin is flipped, \(P(H)=\frac12\), because there is one desired outcome, Heads, out of two possible outcomes.

b) Empirical probability

Empirical probability describes what actually happens in an experiment. Suppose a coin is flipped 500 times and Heads occurs 257 times. Then,

$$P(H)=\frac{257}{500}=0.514$$

Thus, the experimental probability of obtaining Heads is 0.514 or 51.4%.

c) Law of Large Numbers

The Law of Large Numbers states that as the number of trials increases, the experimental probability tends to get closer to the theoretical probability. For example, the theoretical probability of getting Heads is \(0.5\). If a coin is flipped many times, the proportion of Heads obtained should gradually approach \(0.5\).

Compound probabilities

A compound probability involves two or more events. We may want to know the probability that two events happen together, that either event happens, or that one event happens given that another event has already occurred.

a) Probability of A and B

\(P(A \text{ and } B)\) represents the probability that both events \(A\) and \(B\) occur. For example, suppose event \(A\) is that it is sunny outside and event \(B\) is that it is the weekend. We are interested in the probability that it is sunny and it is also the weekend.

$$P(A\text{ and }B)=P(A)\times P(B\mid A)$$

Here, \(P(B\mid A)\) means the probability of event \(B\) occurring given that event \(A\) has occurred.

b) Probability of A or B

\(P(A \text{ or } B)\) represents the probability that \(A\) occurs, \(B\) occurs, or both occur.

$$P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\text{ and }B)$$

We subtract \(P(A\text{ and }B)\) because outcomes belonging to both events would otherwise be counted twice.

c) Probability of A given B

\(P(A\mid B)\) represents the probability that event \(A\) occurs when we already know that event \(B\) has occurred. In this case, we look only at the outcomes belonging to event \(B\) and determine how many of them also belong to event \(A\).

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\text{ and }B)}{P(B)}$$

Rearranging the "and" rule gives Bayes' rule, where the \(A_i\) partition \(\Omega\) (law of total probability):

$$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)=\sum_i P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i)$$

Example — rolling a die

Consider a fair die numbered from 1 to 6. The possible outcomes are:

$$\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}$$

a) Probability of rolling a prime number

The prime numbers on the die are:

$$\{2,3,5\}$$

There are three prime numbers out of six possible outcomes. Therefore,

$$P(\text{prime})=\frac{3}{6}=\frac{1}{2}=0.5=50\%$$

Thus, there is a 50% probability of rolling a prime number.

b) Probability of rolling a prime or an odd number

The prime numbers are \(\{2,3,5\}\) and the odd numbers are \(\{1,3,5\}\). The numbers 3 and 5 belong to both groups. Using the addition rule,

$$P(\text{prime or odd})=P(\text{prime})+P(\text{odd})-P(\text{prime and odd})$$

Therefore,

$$P(\text{prime or odd})=\frac{3}{6}+\frac{3}{6}-\frac{2}{6}=\frac{4}{6}=\frac{2}{3}$$

Thus, the probability of rolling either a prime number or an odd number is \(\frac{2}{3}\).

c) Probability of rolling a prime and odd number

The numbers that are both prime and odd are:

$$\{3,5\}$$

Using the multiplication rule,

$$P(\text{prime and odd})=P(\text{prime})\times P(\text{odd}\mid\text{prime})$$

Among the three prime numbers \(2,3,5\), two of them are odd. Therefore,

$$P(\text{odd}\mid\text{prime})=\frac{2}{3}$$

Hence,

$$P(\text{prime and odd})=\frac{3}{6}\times\frac{2}{3}=\frac{6}{18}=\frac{1}{3}$$

Thus, the probability of rolling a number that is both prime and odd is \(\frac13\).

d) Probability of rolling a prime given that the number is odd

Here, we already know that the number rolled is odd. Therefore, we consider only the odd outcomes \(\{1,3,5\}\). Among these three outcomes, 3 and 5 are prime.

$$P(\text{prime}\mid\text{odd})=\frac{P(\text{prime and odd})}{P(\text{odd})}=\frac{\frac13}{\frac36}=\frac{2}{3}$$

Thus, given that the number rolled is odd, the probability that it is also prime is \(\frac23\).

Example — choosing a blue card

Suppose there are five cards numbered 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Among them, the blue cards are numbered 1 and 2 (the even cards are 2 and 4).

a) Probability of choosing a blue card

There are 2 blue cards among 5 cards.

$$P(\text{Blue})=\frac25=0.4$$

b) Probability of choosing a blue and even card

Only one card satisfies both conditions.

$$P(\text{Blue and Even})=\frac15=0.2$$

c) Probability of choosing a blue or even card

Using the OR rule,

$$P(\text{Blue or Even})=P(\text{Blue})+P(\text{Even})-P(\text{Blue and Even})=\frac25+\frac25-\frac15=\frac35=0.6$$

Drawing two cards without replacement

Suppose two cards are drawn without replacement. Because the first card is not returned before the second card is selected, the total number of available cards decreases after the first selection. For example,

$$P(B_1\text{ and }B_2)=\frac25\times\frac14=\frac{2}{20}=\frac{1}{10}$$

The important idea is that drawing without replacement changes the probability of the second event.

Independent events

Two events are independent when the occurrence of one event does not change the probability of the other event occurring. There are two useful ways to check independence:

$$P(A\mid B)=P(A)\qquad\text{or}\qquad P(B\mid A)=P(B)$$
Independent events. $$P(A\text{ and }B)=P(A)\,P(B)$$

Example

Suppose 20% of students are left-handed and 5% of students are carrying an A. Then \(P(L)=0.20\) and \(P(A)=0.05\). If being left-handed and carrying an A are independent,

$$P(L\text{ and }A)=P(L)P(A)=(0.20)(0.05)=0.01$$

Thus, the probability of a randomly selected student being both left-handed and carrying an A is 1%.

Mutually exclusive events

Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot occur at the same time.

Mutually exclusive events. $$P(A\text{ and }B)=0$$ so the OR rule reduces to \(P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)\).

Example: rolling a die

Suppose a fair die is rolled. Let \(A=\{\text{numbers less than 4}\}=\{1,2,3\}\) and \(B=\{\text{numbers greater than 3}\}=\{4,5,6\}\). There are no outcomes common to both sets. Therefore,

$$P(A\text{ and }B)=0$$

Hence, \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive events.

Venn diagrams

A Venn diagram uses overlapping circles to show the relationships between different groups or sets. Each circle represents a group, while the overlapping regions represent people who belong to two or more groups. In this example, the three circles represent people who use Netflix, Hulu, and Cable.

The diagram shows that 15 people use only Netflix, 13 use only Hulu, and 10 use only Cable. The overlapping regions show that 21 people use Netflix and Hulu, 7 use Netflix and Cable, and 6 use Hulu and Cable. The central region shows that 11 people use all three services. The 4 people outside the circles do not use Netflix, Hulu, or Cable. The total number of people is:

$$n=87$$

a) Probability that a person uses Hulu

$$P(\text{Hulu})=\frac{21+11+6+13}{87}=\frac{51}{87}\approx0.59$$

Thus, the probability that a randomly selected person uses Hulu is approximately 59%.

b) Probability that a person uses Hulu or Netflix

The word "or" means that the person may use Hulu, Netflix, or both. Therefore, we include every region that belongs to either the Hulu circle or the Netflix circle. The relevant values are:

$$15,\;21,\;13,\;7,\;11,\;6$$

Therefore,

$$P(\text{Hulu or Netflix})=\frac{15+21+13+7+11+6}{87}=\frac{73}{87}\approx0.84$$

Thus, the probability that a randomly selected person uses Hulu or Netflix is approximately 84%.

c) Probability that a person uses Hulu and Netflix

The word "and" means that the person must belong to both the Hulu and Netflix circles. There are two regions where Hulu and Netflix overlap:

  • 21 people use Hulu and Netflix but not Cable.
  • 11 people use Hulu, Netflix, and Cable.

Therefore,

$$P(\text{Hulu and Netflix})=\frac{21+11}{87}=\frac{32}{87}\approx0.37$$

Thus, the probability that a randomly selected person uses both Hulu and Netflix is approximately 37%.

d) Probability of using Hulu given that the person uses Netflix

The notation \(P(H\mid N)\) means the probability that a person uses Hulu given that we already know the person uses Netflix. Since we know the person uses Netflix, we consider only the people inside the Netflix circle. The total number of Netflix users is:

$$15+21+7+11=54$$

Among these Netflix users, the people who also use Hulu are \(21+11=32\). Therefore,

$$P(H\mid N)=\frac{21+11}{15+21+7+11}=\frac{32}{54}\approx0.59$$

Thus, given that a person uses Netflix, the probability that the person also uses Hulu is approximately 59%.

Tree diagrams and Bayes' theorem

A tree diagram visually organizes conditional probabilities. Each branch represents a possible outcome, and the probabilities along the branches can be multiplied to calculate the probability of a complete sequence of events.

The next example begins with a jar containing 5 blue marbles and 3 green marbles. Two marbles are drawn without replacement, so the probability on the second draw depends on what happened on the first draw.

Multiplying along each path gives the probability of that sequence:

$$P(BB)=\frac58\cdot\frac47=\frac{5}{14},\quad P(BG)=\frac58\cdot\frac37=\frac{15}{56},\quad P(GB)=\frac38\cdot\frac57=\frac{15}{56},\quad P(GG)=\frac38\cdot\frac27=\frac{3}{28}$$

The four paths cover every possibility, so they sum to 1. Bayes' theorem reverses the conditioning. Given that the second marble is green, what is the probability the first was blue? Adding the paths that end in green gives \(P(G_2)=\frac{15}{56}+\frac{6}{56}=\frac38\), so

$$P(B_1\mid G_2)=\frac{P(B_1\text{ and }G_2)}{P(G_2)}=\frac{15/56}{21/56}=\frac57$$

Bayes' rule

Bayes' rule is used to find a conditional probability in the reverse direction. For example, if we know \(P(Y\mid X)\), Bayes' rule can be used to find \(P(X\mid Y)\):

Bayes' rule. $$P(X\mid Y)=\frac{P(X)\,P(Y\mid X)}{P(Y)}$$ In general, $$P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A)\,P(B\mid A)}{P(B)}$$

Example — hard and easy courses

Suppose students take either a Hard course or an Easy course. The probabilities are:

CourseProbability of taking the courseProbability of failing the course
Hard0.20.1
Easy0.80.005

Let \(H\) = the student takes a Hard course, \(E\) = the student takes an Easy course, and \(F\) = the student fails the course. We are given

$$P(H)=0.2,\quad P(E)=0.8,\quad P(F\mid H)=0.1,\quad P(F\mid E)=0.005$$

We want \(P(H\mid F)\): given that a student failed, what is the probability that the student was taking a Hard course?

Step 1: find the overall probability of failing

A student can fail either while taking a Hard course or while taking an Easy course. Therefore,

$$P(F)=P(F\mid H)P(H)+P(F\mid E)P(E)=(0.1)(0.2)+(0.005)(0.8)=0.02+0.004=0.024$$

Step 2: apply Bayes' rule

$$P(H\mid F)=\frac{P(H)P(F\mid H)}{P(F)}=\frac{(0.2)(0.1)}{0.024}=\frac{0.02}{0.024}\approx0.8333$$

Thus, given that a student failed the course, there is approximately an 83.33% probability that the student was taking a Hard course.

Bayes' theorem: general formula

When an event \(B\) can occur through several mutually exclusive possibilities \(A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_n\), Bayes' theorem can be written as

$$P(A_i\mid B)=\frac{P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(A_j)P(B\mid A_j)}$$

For the example above,

$$P(H\mid F)=\frac{P(H)P(F\mid H)}{P(H)P(F\mid H)+P(E)P(F\mid E)}=\frac{(0.2)(0.1)}{(0.2)(0.1)+(0.8)(0.005)}\approx0.8333$$

Random variables

Random variable. A function \(X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\) that assigns a number to each outcome. Capital \(X\) is the variable; lowercase \(x\) is a value it can take.

A discrete variable takes countably many values (a die roll, a coin flip count). A continuous variable takes values in an interval (a waiting time, a sensor reading). Discrete variables are described by a mass function, continuous ones by a density; the CDF works for both.

Representing outcomes with numbers

A random variable is useful for representing the outcomes of a random experiment using numbers. For example, when tossing a coin we may assign \(0\rightarrow\text{Head}\) and \(1\rightarrow\text{Tail}\). Numbers can also be assigned when the outcomes are categorical:

Thus, the variable that associates a number with an outcome of a random experiment is called a random variable. It is usually written as a capital letter such as \(X\), while a particular value is written as a small letter such as \(x\). For example, \(X=5\) means that the random variable \(X\) has taken the value 5.

Example: rainfall. Let \(R\) be the amount of rainfall on a particular day. The question "What is the probability that the rainfall is greater than 10 mm?" is written mathematically as \(P(R\gt10)\).

Definition. A random variable is a function that assigns a real number to each element in the sample space of a random experiment.

Discrete random variables

Discrete random variable. A random variable that can take at most a countable number of possible values. In other words, a real-valued function defined on a discrete sample space.

Examples: the number of heads obtained when coins are tossed, the number of defective items in a batch, the number of phone calls received, the number of students in a classroom.

Continuous random variables

Continuous random variable. If the range of a random variable \(X\) contains an interval of real numbers, finite or infinite, then \(X\) is called a continuous random variable.

For example, let \(Y\) be the time required to accomplish a task. If the task always takes between 2 and 3 minutes, then \(2\leq Y\leq3\) and the sample space is the interval \([2,3]\). There are infinitely many possible values within this interval, so \(Y\) is continuous. Continuous random variables take values from an uncountably infinite sample space, such as an interval of real numbers.

Discrete vs. continuous random variables

In practical problems, continuous random variables usually represent measured data: height, weight, temperature, time, distance. Discrete random variables usually represent count data: the number of defective items, students, calls or accidents. The arrival time of a train at a specified stop and the lifetime of a transistor can be represented by continuous random variables.

Probability mass function (PMF)

PMF. For discrete \(X\), \(\;p_X(x)=P(X=x)\), with \(p_X(x)\ge 0\) and \(\sum_x p_X(x)=1\).

Probabilities of sets add up: \(P(X\in S)=\sum_{x\in S}p_X(x)\). Examples: a fair die has \(p(x)=\tfrac16\) for \(x=1,\dots,6\); a Bernoulli(\(q\)) variable has \(p(1)=q,\ p(0)=1-q\); a Binomial(\(n,q\)) variable has

$$p(k)=\binom{n}{k}q^k(1-q)^{n-k},\quad k=0,\dots,n.$$

Characteristics of a probability distribution

A probability distribution describes how probabilities are distributed among the possible values of a random variable. A valid distribution must satisfy two conditions:

  • Each probability lies between 0 and 1: \(0\leq P(x)\leq1\).
  • The probabilities sum to 1: \(\sum P(x)=1\).

PMF: formal definition and properties

Suppose \(X\) is a one-dimensional discrete random variable taking a countable number of values \(x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots\). With each possible value \(x_i\) we associate a probability \(p_i\):

$$P(X=x_i)=p(x_i)=p_i$$

The function \(p(x_i)\) is called the probability mass function (PMF), or probability function, of \(X\).

  • Property 1 (non-negative): \(p(x_i)\geq0\) for every \(x_i\); in fact \(0\leq p(x_i)\leq1\).
  • Property 2 (sums to 1): \(\sum_i p(x_i)=1\).

The set of values that \(X\) can take is called the spectrum of the random variable.

Probability density function (PDF)

PDF. For continuous \(X\), \(f_X(x)\ge 0\), \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_X(x)\,dx=1\), and $$P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^b f_X(x)\,dx.$$

Probability is the area under the curve. A density is not a probability: \(P(X=x)=0\) for every single point, and \(f_X(x)\) can exceed 1. Two standard cases are the uniform density \(f(x)=\frac{1}{b-a}\) on \([a,b]\) and the Gaussian

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).$$

Gaussian distribution

Gaussian (normal) density. $$\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(x-\mu)^2\right)$$ where \(\mu\) is the mean and \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation.

Example: heights of college students

The heights of students in a college follow a Gaussian distribution with a mean of 170 cm and a standard deviation of 5 cm. Find the value of the Gaussian probability density at a height of 175 cm.

Given \(\mu=170\), \(\sigma=5\), \(x=175\), and the density

$$f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left[-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]$$

Substituting the values,

$$f(175)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(5)^2}}\exp\left[-\frac{(175-170)^2}{2(5)^2}\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{50\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{25}{50}\right)=\frac{1}{5\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-0.5}$$

Using \(e^{-0.5}\approx0.6065\) and \(\sqrt{2\pi}\approx2.5066\),

$$f(175)=\frac{0.6065}{5(2.5066)}\approx0.0484$$

Therefore, the Gaussian probability density at \(x=175\) cm is approximately 0.0484.

0.0484 is a probability density, not a 4.84% probability. To find the probability that height lies within a range, we calculate the area under the Gaussian curve over that range.

PDF: formal definition and properties

Let \(X\) be a continuous random variable. If there exists a non-negative function \(f(x)\), defined for all real \(x\), such that for any set \(B\) of real numbers

$$P(X\in B)=\int_B f(x)\,dx$$

then \(f(x)\) is called the probability density function (PDF) of \(X\). For an interval, \(P(a\lt X\lt b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), so probability is the area under the PDF curve between \(a\) and \(b\). A valid PDF satisfies:

  • Property 1 (non-negative): \(f(x)\geq0\) for every \(x\).
  • Property 2 (total area is 1): \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1\).
  • Property 3 (interval probability): \(P(a\lt X\lt b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), the area under \(f(x)\) between two values of \(x\).

Probability at a single point

An important property of a continuous random variable is \(P(X=a)=0\) for every particular value \(a\). This does not mean that \(X\) cannot take the value \(a\); it means that probability for a continuous random variable is determined over an interval rather than at an individual point. Therefore \(P(a\lt X\lt b)=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\).

Uniform distribution example

Suppose the time required to complete a task is always between 2 and 3 minutes and every time in this interval is equally likely. Then \(X\) has a uniform distribution over \([2,3]\). The interval has width \(3-2=1\), so the PDF has constant height 1:

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}1, & 2\leq x\leq3\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

The probability that the task takes between 2.4 and 2.6 minutes is the shaded area under the PDF:

$$P(2.4\lt X\lt2.6)=\int_{2.4}^{2.6}1\,dx=[x]_{2.4}^{2.6}=2.6-2.4=0.2$$

Continuous random variables and PDF conditions

A continuous random variable can take any value in an interval of the real number line. For example, \(X\in[a,b]\) means \(X\) can take any real value between \(a\) and \(b\). Unlike a discrete random variable, which has a countable number of possible values, a continuous random variable has an uncountable number of possible values.

A function \(f(x)\) is the probability density function of \(X\) if it satisfies three conditions:

  • Integrable over \((-\infty,\infty)\).
  • Non-negative: \(f(x)\geq0\) for all \(x\).
  • Total area equal to 1: \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1\).

Thus, the total area under a probability density function is always equal to 1.

Example 1 — finding the constant and a probability

A continuous random variable \(X\) has PDF \(f(x)=Ax^2\) for \(0\leq x\leq1\), and \(f(x)=0\) otherwise. Find \(A\) and \(P(0.2\leq X\leq0.5)\).

Step 1: find A

Since the PDF is nonzero only for \(0\leq x\leq1\),

$$\int_0^1Ax^2\,dx=A\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^1=\frac A3(1-0)=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad A=3$$

Thus, the PDF becomes \(f(x)=3x^2\) for \(0\leq x\leq1\), and 0 otherwise.

Step 2: find P(0.2 ≤ X ≤ 0.5)

$$P(0.2\leq X\leq0.5)=\int_{0.2}^{0.5}3x^2\,dx=3\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{0.2}^{0.5}=[x^3]_{0.2}^{0.5}=(0.5)^3-(0.2)^3=0.125-0.008=0.117$$

Cumulative distribution function (CDF)

CDF. \(F_X(x)=P(X\le x)\), which equals \(\sum_{t\le x}p_X(t)\) for discrete \(X\) and \(\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(t)\,dt\) for continuous \(X\).

(The syllabus says "cumulative density function"; the standard name is cumulative distribution function.) Properties: \(F\) is non-decreasing and right-continuous, \(F(-\infty)=0\), \(F(\infty)=1\), and \(P(a\lt X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)\). For continuous \(X\), \(f_X(x)=F_X'(x)\).

Example: the exponential density \(f(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) for \(x\ge 0\) has \(F(x)=1-e^{-\lambda x}\).

Discrete distribution function

For a discrete random variable \(X\) with PMF \(p(x_i)\), the distribution function is defined by adding the probabilities of the values up to \(x\):

$$F(x)=P(X\leq x)=\sum_{x_i\leq x}p(x_i)$$

CDF of a continuous random variable

Let \(X\) be a continuous random variable. The CDF is \(F(x)=P(X\leq x)=P(-\infty\lt X\leq x)\), and for a continuous random variable

$$F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt$$

where \(f(t)\) is the PDF. Therefore, the CDF represents the total probability accumulated from \(-\infty\) up to \(x\).

Properties of the PDF and CDF

  • 1. \(f(x)\geq0\).
  • 2. \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1\).
  • 3. Closed interval: \(P(x_1\leq X\leq x_2)=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}f(x)\,dx\).
  • 4. Open interval: \(P(x_1\lt X\lt x_2)=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}f(x)\,dx\). For a continuous random variable, open and closed interval probabilities are the same.
  • 5. Single point: \(P(X=a)=\int_a^af(x)\,dx=0\).
  • 6. PDF from CDF: \(f(x)=F'(x)\); the PDF is obtained by differentiating the CDF.
  • 7. Probability from the CDF: since \(P(a\leq X\leq b)=\int_a^bf(x)\,dx\) and \(F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt\), $$P(a\leq X\leq b)=F(b)-F(a)$$

Example 2 — finding k and the CDF

A continuous random variable has PDF \(f(x)=k(x-1)^3\) for \(1\leq x\leq3\), and 0 otherwise. Find \(k\) and the CDF of \(X\).

Step 1: find k

The PDF is nonzero only between 1 and 3, so

$$\int_1^3k(x-1)^3\,dx=k\left[\frac{(x-1)^4}{4}\right]_1^3=\frac k4\left[(3-1)^4-(1-1)^4\right]=\frac{16k}{4}=4k=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad k=\frac14$$

The PDF is therefore \(f(x)=\frac14(x-1)^3\) for \(1\leq x\leq3\), and 0 otherwise.

Step 2: find the CDF

The CDF is \(F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f(t)\,dt\). We consider three cases.

  • Case 1, \(x\lt1\): the PDF is zero below 1, so \(F(x)=0\).
  • Case 2, \(1\leq x\lt3\): $$F(x)=\int_1^x\frac14(t-1)^3\,dt=\frac14\left[\frac{(t-1)^4}{4}\right]_1^x=\frac1{16}\left[(x-1)^4-0\right]=\frac{(x-1)^4}{16}$$
  • Case 3, \(x\geq3\): the entire probability has been accumulated, so \(F(x)=1\).

Hence, the complete CDF is

$$F(x)=\begin{cases}0, & x\lt1\\[5pt] \dfrac{(x-1)^4}{16}, & 1\leq x\lt3\\[7pt] 1, & x\geq3\end{cases}$$

Example 3 — CDF given directly

The CDF of a continuous random variable \(X\) is

$$F(x)=\begin{cases}0, & x\leq0\\ C(1-e^{-x}), & x\gt0\end{cases}$$

Find \(C\) and \(P(1\lt X\lt2)\).

Find the PDF from the CDF

Since \(f(x)=F'(x)\): for \(x\leq0\), \(F(x)=0\) so \(f(x)=0\). For \(x\gt0\), using \(\frac{d}{dx}(1-e^{-x})=e^{-x}\),

$$f(x)=C\frac{d}{dx}(1-e^{-x})=Ce^{-x}$$

so \(f(x)=0\) for \(x\leq0\) and \(f(x)=Ce^{-x}\) for \(x\gt0\).

Find C

A valid PDF must integrate to 1:

$$\int_0^\infty Ce^{-x}\,dx=C\left[-e^{-x}\right]_0^\infty=-C[e^{-\infty}-e^0]=-C(0-1)=C=1$$

Hence \(C=1\), so \(F(x)=1-e^{-x}\) for \(x\gt0\) (and 0 for \(x\leq0\)), and \(f(x)=e^{-x}\) for \(x\gt0\) (and 0 for \(x\leq0\)).

Find P(1 < X < 2)

$$P(1\lt X\lt2)=\int_1^2e^{-x}\,dx=[-e^{-x}]_1^2=-e^{-2}+e^{-1}=\frac1e-\frac1{e^2}$$

Using \(\frac1e\approx0.3679\) and \(\frac1{e^2}\approx0.1353\), we get approximately \(P(1\lt X\lt2)\approx0.2325\).

Expectation

Expectation. $$E[X]=\sum_x x\,p_X(x)\qquad\text{or}\qquad E[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,f_X(x)\,dx$$

It is the probability-weighted average of the values. For a fair die, \(E[X]=\frac{1+2+\cdots+6}{6}=3.5\), a value the die can never show. Key facts:

  • Function of \(X\): \(E[g(X)]=\sum_x g(x)p_X(x)\) (or \(\int g(x)f_X(x)dx\)), with no need to find the distribution of \(g(X)\).
  • Linearity: \(E[aX+bY+c]=aE[X]+bE[Y]+c\), always, even when \(X,Y\) are dependent.
  • Variance: \(\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[(X-\mu)^2]=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2\). For the die, \(\frac{91}{6}-3.5^2=\frac{35}{12}\).

Mean and variance of a discrete random variable

The mean, or expected value, gives the expected or average value of the random variable:

$$E(X)=\sum_x x\,p(x)$$

The variance is

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\sum_x x^2p(x)-\left[\sum_x xp(x)\right]^2=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2,\qquad E(X^2)=\sum_x x^2p(x)$$

Expected value: discrete and continuous

The expected value, or mean, gives the average value of a random variable. We multiply each possible value by its probability and add the results. Written with \(p(x)\) for the density in the continuous case,

$$E[X]=\sum_i P(x_i)\,x_i\qquad\qquad E(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x)\,x\,dx$$

Example — expected value of a die roll

For a fair six-sided die, \(P(X=x)=\frac16\) for \(x=1,\ldots,6\). Therefore,

$$E(X)=1\left(\tfrac16\right)+2\left(\tfrac16\right)+3\left(\tfrac16\right)+4\left(\tfrac16\right)+5\left(\tfrac16\right)+6\left(\tfrac16\right)=\frac16(1+2+3+4+5+6)=\frac{21}{6}=3.5$$

This does not mean that a single die roll can produce 3.5. Rather, 3.5 is the average value expected over many rolls.

Variance and standard deviation

Variance tells us how far the possible values of a random variable are spread out from their expected value.

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=E\left[(X-E(X))^2\right]=\sum_i P(x_i)\,(x_i-E(X))^2,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$$

Example: variance of a die roll

We found \(E(X)=3.5\), and every outcome has probability \(\frac16\). Therefore,

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac16\left[(1-3.5)^2+(2-3.5)^2+(3-3.5)^2+(4-3.5)^2+(5-3.5)^2+(6-3.5)^2\right]$$ $$=\frac16[6.25+2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25+6.25]=\frac{17.5}{6}\approx2.917$$

For the standard deviation, \(\sigma=\sqrt{2.917}\approx1.7\).

Mean, second moment and variance of a continuous random variable

Binomial distribution

A binomial distribution gives the probability of obtaining a particular number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials.

Characteristics

  • Fixed number of trials: the number of trials \(n\) is determined in advance.
  • Two possible outcomes: each trial is a success or a failure.
  • Constant probability: the probability of success \(p\) is the same for every trial. The probability of failure is \(q=1-p\).
Binomial random variable. \(X\sim B(n,p)\), where \(n\) is the number of trials, \(p\) the probability of success and \(X\) the number of successes. $$\mu=np\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{npq}$$ $$P(X=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^xq^{n-x},\qquad \binom{n}{r}=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!\,r!}$$

Success, failure and the probability function

A binomial distribution is used when a random experiment has only two possible outcomes, usually called success and failure. Let \(p=P(\text{success})\) and \(q=P(\text{failure})\). Since these are the only two possible outcomes,

$$p+q=1\qquad\text{or}\qquad q=1-p$$

Suppose the experiment is performed \(n\) times independently, and let \(X\) be the number of successes. Then the probability of exactly \(x\) successes is

$$f(x)=P(X=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^xq^{\,n-x},\qquad x=0,1,2,\ldots,n$$

This probability distribution is called the binomial distribution.

Mean, variance and standard deviation

The mean or expected value is \(E(X)=\sum x f(x)\). For a binomial distribution,

$$\mu=E(X)=np$$

For the variance, the notes use \(E(X^2)=\sum x^2f(x)=n(n-1)p^2+np\). Expanding, \(E(X^2)=n^2p^2-np^2+np\). Since \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2\),

$$\operatorname{Var}(X)=n^2p^2-np^2+np-(np)^2=np-np^2=np(1-p)=npq$$

because \((np)^2=n^2p^2\) and \(q=1-p\). The standard deviation is the square root of the variance. Hence, the three important results are:

$$\text{Mean}=np,\qquad\text{Variance}=npq,\qquad\text{Standard deviation}=\sqrt{npq}$$

Binomial PDF and CDF

The binomial PDF gives the probability of exactly \(x\) successes; the binomial CDF gives the probability of up to and including \(x\) successes. The notes give the calculator formats as \(\text{binomialpdf}(n,p,x)\) and \(\text{binomialcdf}(n,p,x)\).

Example — carpooling to work

In Honolulu, 7.9% of workers carpool to work. A random sample of 41 workers is selected, so \(n=41\) and \(p=0.079\).

a) Identify the distribution

$$X\sim B(41,0.079)$$

b) Expected number who carpool

$$\mu=np=41(0.079)=3.239$$

We would expect approximately 3.239 workers out of 41 to carpool to work.

c) Standard deviation

First, \(q=1-0.079=0.921\). Then,

$$\sigma=\sqrt{npq}=\sqrt{41(0.079)(0.921)}\approx1.727$$

d) Probability that exactly 3 workers carpool

$$P(X=3)=\text{binomialpdf}(41,0.079,3)\approx0.2304$$

e) Probability that fewer than the expected number carpool

The expected value is 3.239. Since the number of workers must be a whole number, "less than 3.239" corresponds to \(X\leq3\). Therefore,

$$P(X\lt3.239)=P(X\leq3)=\text{binomialcdf}(41,0.079,3)\approx0.5917$$

The source notes list 0.5912; recomputing the sum gives 0.5917.

f) Probability that more than 4 workers carpool

Using the complement rule, \(P(X\gt4)=1-P(X\leq4)\). Therefore,

$$P(X\gt4)=1-\text{binomialcdf}(41,0.079,4)\approx0.2205$$

A six-sided die is rolled 12 times. What is the probability of getting a 1 exactly 5 times? Here \(p=\frac16\), \(q=\frac56\), \(n=12\), \(x=5\). Using the binomial formula,

Find the binomial distribution for which the mean is 5 and the variance is 4.

Step 2: use the variance

Example 2 — binomial distribution with mean 4 and variance 2

For a binomial distribution with mean 4 and variance 2, find (a) the probability of at least 2 successes, (b) the probability of at most 2 successes, and (c) the probability that \(5\leq X\leq7\).

We have \(np=4\) (1) and \(npq=2\) (2). Dividing (2) by (1), \(q=\frac24=\frac12\). Since \(p+q=1\), \(p=\frac12\). Substituting into (1), \(n\left(\frac12\right)=4\), so \(n=8\). Hence \(n=8\), \(p=\frac12\), \(q=\frac12\), and the distribution is

$$P(X=x)=\binom8x\left(\frac12\right)^x\left(\frac12\right)^{8-x},\qquad x=0,1,\ldots,8$$

(a) At least 2 successes

$$P(X\geq2)=1-[P(X=0)+P(X=1)]=1-\left[\binom80\left(\tfrac12\right)^8+\binom81\left(\tfrac12\right)^8\right]=1-\frac{1+8}{2^8}=1-\frac9{256}=\frac{247}{256}$$

(b) At most 2 successes

"At most 2" means \(X\leq2\). With \(\binom80=1\), \(\binom81=8\), \(\binom82=28\),

$$P(X\leq2)=P(0)+P(1)+P(2)=\left(\frac12\right)^8(1+8+28)=\frac{37}{256}$$

(c) \(5\leq X\leq7\)

With \(\binom85=56\), \(\binom86=28\), \(\binom87=8\),

$$P(5\leq X\leq7)=P(5)+P(6)+P(7)=\frac{56+28+8}{2^8}=\frac{92}{256}$$

A binomial random variable \(X\) satisfies \(9P(X=4)=P(X=2)\) when \(n=6\). Find the parameter \(p\). With \(P(X=x)=\binom6xp^xq^{6-x}\),

Poisson distribution

A Poisson distribution models the number of times an event occurs within a specified interval.

Characteristics

  • We are interested in the number of successes in a fixed interval.
  • We know the average number of successes in that interval, written \(\mu\).
Poisson random variable. \(X\sim P(\mu)\), where \(\mu\) is the average number of occurrences during the interval. Its mean is \(\mu\) and its standard deviation is \(\sqrt\mu\). $$P(X=x)=\frac{\mu^x e^{-\mu}}{x!},\qquad e\approx2.71828$$

Poisson as a limit of the binomial

The Poisson distribution can be considered a limiting case of the binomial distribution. When the number of trials \(n\) becomes very large and the probability of success \(p\) becomes very small, such that \(np=\lambda\) remains constant, the binomial distribution approaches the Poisson distribution. That is,

$$n\rightarrow\infty,\qquad p\rightarrow0,\qquad np=\lambda$$

where \(\lambda\) is a constant. (The rest of this section uses \(\lambda\) for the average number of occurrences; the earlier examples call the same quantity \(\mu\).)

Probability mass function

If \(X\) follows a Poisson distribution with parameter \(\lambda\), then

$$P(X=x)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!},\qquad x=0,1,2,3,\ldots$$

so the probability mass function can be written as

$$P(X=x)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}, & x=0,1,2,\ldots\\[6pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Here \(X\) is the number of occurrences, \(x\) a particular number of occurrences, \(\lambda\) the average number of occurrences, and \(e\) the mathematical constant.

Mean, variance and standard deviation

For a Poisson distribution the mean is \(E(X)=\lambda\), and the variance is also \(\lambda\). Since \(\sigma^2=\lambda\), the standard deviation is \(\sigma=\sqrt\lambda\). The notes also give \(E(X^2)=\lambda^2+\lambda\). The important results are:

$$\text{Mean}=\text{Variance}=\lambda,\qquad\text{S.D.}=\sqrt\lambda$$

Poisson PDF and CDF

The Poisson PDF, \(\text{poissonpdf}(\mu,x)\), gives the probability of exactly \(x\) occurrences. The Poisson CDF, \(\text{poissoncdf}(\mu,x)\), gives the probability of up to and including \(x\) occurrences.

Example — babies born per week

A hospital baby unit has an average of 4 births per week, so \(\mu=4\) and \(X\sim P(4)\).

a) Expected value and standard deviation

$$\mu=4,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt4=2$$

b) Exactly 4 babies are born

$$P(X=4)=\text{poissonpdf}(4,4)\approx0.1954$$

c) Fewer than 4 births

Since \(X\) is discrete, \(P(X\lt4)=P(X\leq3)\). Therefore,

$$P(X\lt4)=\text{poissoncdf}(4,3)\approx0.4335$$

d) 7 or more births

Using the complement rule, \(P(X\geq7)=1-P(X\leq6)\). Therefore,

$$P(X\geq7)=1-\text{poissoncdf}(4,6)\approx0.1107$$

$$P(X=8)=\frac{12^8e^{-12}}{8!}\approx0.065523\approx6.55\%$$

A student receives an average of 7 text messages in a 2-hour period.

Example 1 — finding the mean from given probabilities

If a random variable has a Poisson distribution such that \(P(1)=P(2)\), find the mean of the distribution and \(P(4)\). Using \(P(X=x)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!}\) and the condition \(P(X=1)=P(X=2)\),

Example 2 — car-hire firm

A car-hire firm has 2 cars, which it hires out on a day-to-day basis. The number of demands for a car on each day follows a Poisson distribution with mean \(\lambda=1.5\). Calculate the proportion of days on which there is no demand, and on which some demand is refused.

Example 4 — finding the standard deviation

If \(X\) is a Poisson random variable such that \(P(X=2)=9P(X=4)+90P(X=6)\), find the standard deviation. Using the condition,

$$\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^2}{2!}=9\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^4}{4!}+90\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^6}{6!}$$

Cancel \(e^{-\lambda}\) and simplify the coefficients:

$$\frac{\lambda^2}{2}=\frac{3\lambda^4}{8}+\frac{\lambda^6}{8}$$

Multiplying throughout by 8 gives \(4\lambda^2=3\lambda^4+\lambda^6\). Rearranging and factorizing,

$$\lambda^6+3\lambda^4-4\lambda^2=0\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda^2(\lambda^4+3\lambda^2-4)=0\;\Rightarrow\;\lambda^2(\lambda^2+4)(\lambda^2-1)=0$$

This gives three cases. Case 1: \(\lambda^2=0\) is not taken as the required solution in the notes. Case 2: \(\lambda^2=-4\) does not give a real positive \(\lambda\). Case 3: \(\lambda^2=1\), so \(\lambda=\pm1\); since the Poisson parameter must be positive, the negative value is rejected. Hence \(\lambda=1\).

Finding the standard deviation

For a Poisson distribution, the variance is \(\lambda\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=1\) and

$$\sigma=\sqrt\lambda=\sqrt1=1$$

Therefore, the standard deviation of the Poisson distribution is 1.

Poisson distribution: quick summary

Summary. A Poisson distribution is the limiting case of the binomial distribution as \(n\rightarrow\infty\), \(p\rightarrow0\) with \(np=\lambda\) constant. $$P(X=x)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!},\quad x=0,1,2,\ldots$$ $$E(X)=\lambda,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=\lambda,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt\lambda,\qquad E(X^2)=\lambda^2+\lambda$$ For cumulative probabilities, \(P(X\leq k)=P(0)+P(1)+\cdots+P(k)\). For probabilities greater than a value, use the complement rule: \(P(X\gt k)=1-P(X\leq k)\).

Multinomial distribution

The multinomial distribution is an extension of the binomial distribution to experiments in which each trial can result in more than two possible outcomes. Suppose a trial can result in \(k\) possible outcomes \(E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_k\) with probabilities \(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_k\). Let \(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_k\) be the number of occurrences of \(E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_k\) in \(n\) independent trials. Then the probability distribution is called a multinomial distribution. On each trial the events are mutually exclusive.

Multinomial probability formula. $$P(X_1=x_1,\ldots,X_k=x_k)=\frac{n!}{x_1!\,x_2!\cdots x_k!}\,p_1^{x_1}p_2^{x_2}\cdots p_k^{x_k}$$ where \(\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_i=n\) and \(\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_i=1\), that is, \(x_1+\cdots+x_k=n\) and \(p_1+\cdots+p_k=1\). The multinomial coefficient is $$\binom{n}{x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_k}=\frac{n!}{x_1!\,x_2!\cdots x_k!}$$

Relation to the binomial distribution

The binomial distribution has only two outcomes, typically success and failure, with \(P(X=x)=\binom nxp^xq^{n-x}\) and \(q=1-p\). The multinomial distribution generalizes this to \(k\) outcomes:

$$P(X_1=x_1,\ldots,X_k=x_k)=\frac{n!}{x_1!\,x_2!\cdots x_k!}\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_i^{x_i}$$

Example 1 — rolling a die six times

A die is rolled 6 times. Find the probability that each of the six faces appears exactly once.

Step 1: identify the number of trials

The die is rolled six times, so \(n=6\). There are six outcomes, and for a fair die \(p_1=\cdots=p_6=\frac16\). Check: \(\sum p_i=\frac66=1\).

Step 2: determine the number of occurrences

We want each face exactly once, so \(x_1=\cdots=x_6=1\). Check: \(\sum x_i=6=n\).

Step 3: apply the multinomial formula

$$P=\frac{6!}{1!\,1!\,1!\,1!\,1!\,1!}\left(\frac16\right)^6=\frac{6!}{(1!)^6}\left(\frac16\right)^6=720\left(\frac1{6^6}\right)=\frac{720}{46656}\approx0.015432$$

Thus, the probability that each face appears exactly once in six rolls is approximately 0.015432, or about 1.5432%.

Example 2 — drawing coloured balls with replacement

A box contains 4 white, 3 black and 5 red balls. A ball is drawn at random, its colour is noted, and the ball is replaced. This is repeated until 6 balls have been drawn. Find the probability that (i) 3 are white, 1 is black and 2 are red, and (ii) 2 are white and 4 are red.

Step 1: find the individual probabilities

The total number of balls is \(4+3+5=12\). Because the ball is replaced after every draw, the six trials are independent and the probabilities remain constant:

$$p_W=\frac4{12}=\frac13,\qquad p_B=\frac3{12}=\frac14,\qquad p_R=\frac5{12}$$

Check: \(\frac13+\frac14+\frac5{12}=\frac4{12}+\frac3{12}+\frac5{12}=1\).

$$P=\frac{6!}{3!\,1!\,2!}\left(\frac13\right)^3\left(\frac14\right)^1\left(\frac5{12}\right)^2=\frac{720}{6\times1\times2}\times\frac1{27}\times\frac14\times\frac{25}{144}=60\times\frac1{27}\times\frac14\times\frac{25}{144}$$ $$P=\frac{125}{1296}\approx0.09645$$

Thus, the probability of obtaining 3 white, 1 black and 2 red balls is approximately 9.65%.

(ii) 2 white and 4 red

$$P=15\times\frac19\times\frac{625}{20736}=\frac{3125}{62208}\approx0.05024$$

Thus, the probability of obtaining 2 white and 4 red balls is approximately 5.02%.

\(x_1=2\), \(x_2=1\), \(x_3=3\); check \(2+1+3=6=n\).

With denominator 18, \(\frac29+\frac16+\frac{11}{18}=\frac4{18}+\frac3{18}+\frac{11}{18}=\frac{18}{18}=1\), so the probabilities form a valid distribution.

Step 3: apply the multinomial formula

The multinomial coefficient is \(\frac{6!}{2!\,1!\,3!}=\frac{720}{2\times1\times6}=60\). Therefore,

using \(11^3=1331\) and \(18^3=5832\). This simplifies to

When to use the multinomial distribution

  • There is a fixed number of trials, \(n\).
  • Each trial has one of \(k\) possible outcomes, and the outcomes are mutually exclusive for an individual trial.
  • The trials are independent, and the probability of each outcome remains constant from trial to trial.
  • The outcome probabilities satisfy \(p_1+p_2+\cdots+p_k=1\), and the observed frequencies satisfy \(x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_k=n\).

Joint and marginal distributions

For two variables, the joint PMF is \(p(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y)\) and the joint PDF \(f(x,y)\) gives \(P((X,Y)\in R)=\iint_R f\,dx\,dy\). Both are non-negative and total 1. The joint CDF is \(F(x,y)=P(X\le x,Y\le y)\). Everything extends to many variables \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) with \(p(x_1,\dots,x_n)\).

Marginals. Sum or integrate out the other variable: $$p_X(x)=\sum_y p(x,y)\qquad f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy$$

Joint probability from two experiments

When two different experiments or variables are considered together, we often want to know how their outcomes are related. Suppose we roll a die (experiment 1) and flip a coin (experiment 2). Continuous two-dimensional random variables

Jointly continuous. Two continuous random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are jointly continuous if, for any subset \(A\) of the plane corresponding to an event, the probability \(P((X,Y)\in A)\) can be expressed as a double integral over the region \(A\): $$P((X,Y)\in A)=\iint_A f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$$ The function \(f(x,y)\) is real-valued, non-negative and integrable over the entire plane. It is called the joint probability density function (joint PDF) of \(X\) and \(Y\).

The joint PDF describes how probability is distributed over different combinations of \(x\) and \(y\), so probabilities for two continuous random variables are found by integrating it over the required region.

Normalization condition. For \(f(x,y)\) to be a valid joint PDF, the total probability over the entire \(xy\)-plane must equal 1: $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=1$$ For comparison, for a single continuous random variable, \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx=1\).

Example 1 — constant joint PDF

The joint PDF of a two-dimensional random variable is

$$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}c, & 1\lt x\lt3,\;2\lt y\lt4\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Find (i) the value of \(c\), (ii) \(P(1\lt X\lt2,\;3\lt Y\lt4)\), (iii) \(P(X+Y\leq5)\).

(i) Find the value of c

Apply the normalization condition. Since \(f(x,y)=c\) only for \(1\lt x\lt3\), \(2\lt y\lt4\),

$$\int_1^3\int_2^4c\,dy\,dx=1\;\Rightarrow\;c\int_1^3[y]_2^4\,dx=1\;\Rightarrow\;2c\int_1^3dx=1\;\Rightarrow\;2c[x]_1^3=1\;\Rightarrow\;4c=1$$

Hence \(c=\frac14\), and the joint PDF becomes \(f(x,y)=\frac14\) for \(1\lt x\lt3,\;2\lt y\lt4\), and 0 otherwise.

(ii) Find P(1 < X < 2, 3 < Y < 4)

$$P(1\lt X\lt2,\;3\lt Y\lt4)=\int_1^2\int_3^4\frac14\,dy\,dx=\frac14\int_1^2(4-3)\,dx=\frac14[x]_1^2=\frac14(2-1)=\frac14$$

(iii) Find P(X + Y ≤ 5)

The boundary line is \(x+y=5\), with intercepts \((0,5)\) and \((5,0)\). However, the PDF exists only within \(1\lt x\lt3\), \(2\lt y\lt4\). The line passes through this rectangle, and the required region is the triangle below the line (shaded).

Since \(f(x,y)=c=\frac14\) is constant, the probability is \(c\times\) the area of the region. The triangle has base 2 and height 2, so its area is \(\frac12(2)(2)=2\). Thus,

$$P(X+Y\leq5)=\frac14(2)=\frac12$$

Example 2 — joint PDF f(x, y) = kxy

The joint PDF of two continuous random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) is

$$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}kxy, & 0\lt x\lt4,\;1\lt y\lt5\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Find (i) \(k\), (ii) \(P(1\lt X\lt2,\;2\lt Y\lt3)\), (iii) \(P(X\geq3,\;Y\leq4)\), (iv) \(P(X+Y\lt3)\).

(i) Find k

$$\int_0^4\int_1^5kxy\,dy\,dx=1\;\Rightarrow\;\int_0^4kx\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_1^5dx=\int_0^4\frac{kx}{2}(25-1)\,dx=\int_0^4 12kx\,dx=1$$ $$12k\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^4=12k\left(\frac{16}{2}\right)=96k=1$$

Hence \(k=\frac1{96}\), so \(f(x,y)=\frac{xy}{96}\) for \(0\lt x\lt4,\;1\lt y\lt5\), and 0 otherwise.

(ii) Find P(1 < X < 2, 2 < Y < 3)

$$\int_1^2\int_2^3\frac{xy}{96}\,dy\,dx=\int_1^2\frac{x}{96}\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_2^3dx=\int_1^2\frac{x}{192}(3^2-2^2)\,dx=\frac5{192}\int_1^2x\,dx$$ $$=\frac5{192}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_1^2=\frac5{192}\cdot\frac{2^2-1^2}{2}=\frac5{192}\times\frac32=\frac5{128}$$

(iii) Find P(X ≥ 3, Y ≤ 4)

The support of \(X\) is \(0\lt X\lt4\), so \(X\geq3\) restricts \(x\) to \(3\leq x\lt4\). The support of \(Y\) is \(1\lt Y\lt5\), so \(Y\leq4\) gives \(1\lt y\leq4\). Thus,

$$P(X\geq3,\;Y\leq4)=\int_3^4\int_1^4\frac{xy}{96}\,dy\,dx=\int_3^4\frac{x}{192}(4^2-1^2)\,dx=\frac{15}{192}\int_3^4x\,dx$$ $$=\frac{15}{192}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_3^4=\frac{15}{192}\cdot\frac{16-9}{2}=\frac{15}{192}\times\frac72=\frac{35}{128}$$

(iv) Find P(X + Y < 3)

This example requires us to determine the correct integration limits from the region. The boundary is \(x+y=3\), with intercepts \((0,3)\) and \((3,0)\). But the PDF is defined only for \(0\lt x\lt4\), \(1\lt y\lt5\), so the relevant part of the region must also satisfy \(y\gt1\).

Use a vertical strip. For a given \(x\), \(y\) starts at \(y=1\) and extends to \(y=3-x\), so \(1\lt y\lt3-x\). To find the upper limit for \(x\), intersect \(y=1\) with \(x+y=3\): \(x+1=3\), so \(x=2\). Hence the integration limits are

$$0\lt x\lt2,\qquad1\lt y\lt3-x$$

Therefore,

$$P(X+Y\lt3)=\int_0^2\int_1^{3-x}\frac{xy}{96}\,dy\,dx=\int_0^2\frac{x}{192}\left[(3-x)^2-1\right]dx$$

Expanding, \((3-x)^2-1=9-6x+x^2-1=x^2-6x+8\), so

$$=\frac1{192}\int_0^2(x^3-6x^2+8x)\,dx=\frac1{192}\left[\frac{x^4}{4}-2x^3+4x^2\right]_0^2=\frac1{192}[4-16+16]=\frac4{192}=\frac1{48}$$

Continuous joint PDFs: quick summary

Summary. For two jointly continuous random variables, $$P((X,Y)\in A)=\iint_A f(x,y)\,dx\,dy,\qquad f(x,y)\geq0,\qquad\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=1$$ For a rectangular region, $$P(a\lt X\lt b,\;c\lt Y\lt d)=\int_a^b\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx$$ For regions defined by equations such as \(X+Y\lt c\), first identify or sketch the required region, determine the correct \(x\)- and \(y\)-limits from its boundaries, and then evaluate the corresponding double integral.

Joint cumulative distribution function

For two continuous random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), the joint cumulative distribution function gives the probability that \(X\) is less than or equal to \(x\) and \(Y\) is less than or equal to \(y\).

Joint CDF. $$F(x,y)=P(X\leq x,\;Y\leq y)$$ For continuous variables, it is obtained from the joint PDF by integration: $$F(x,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{y}f(u,v)\,dv\,du$$

Example 1 — finding the CDF from a joint PDF

The joint PDF is

$$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}ke^{-(2x+y)}, & x\geq0,\;y\geq0\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Find (i) the value of \(k\), and (ii) the cumulative distribution function \(F(x,y)\).

(i) Find the value of k

The total probability must equal 1. Since the PDF is nonzero only for \(x\geq0,\;y\geq0\), and \(e^{-(2x+y)}=e^{-2x}e^{-y}\),

$$\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty ke^{-(2x+y)}\,dy\,dx=k\int_0^\infty e^{-2x}\left[\int_0^\infty e^{-y}\,dy\right]dx=1$$

Integrating with respect to \(y\): \(\int_0^\infty e^{-y}\,dy=\left[-e^{-y}\right]_0^\infty=-(0-1)=1\), since \(e^{-\infty}=0\) and \(e^0=1\). Therefore \(k\int_0^\infty e^{-2x}\,dx=1\). Integrating with respect to \(x\),

$$k\left[\frac{e^{-2x}}{-2}\right]_0^\infty=-\frac k2\left[0-1\right]=\frac k2=1$$

Hence \(k=2\), and the joint PDF is \(f(x,y)=2e^{-(2x+y)}\) for \(x\geq0,\;y\geq0\), and 0 otherwise.

(ii) Find the cumulative distribution function

By definition \(F(x,y)=P(X\leq x,\;Y\leq y)\). For \(x\geq0\) and \(y\geq0\), using \(u\) and \(v\) as the integration variables,

$$F(x,y)=\int_0^x\int_0^y2e^{-2u}e^{-v}\,dv\,du=\int_0^x2e^{-2u}\left[\int_0^ye^{-v}\,dv\right]du$$

Step 1: integrate with respect to \(v\). \(\int_0^ye^{-v}\,dv=\left[-e^{-v}\right]_0^y=1-e^{-y}\). Thus,

$$F(x,y)=\int_0^x2e^{-2u}(1-e^{-y})\,du=2(1-e^{-y})\int_0^xe^{-2u}\,du$$

since \(1-e^{-y}\) is constant with respect to \(u\).

Step 2: integrate with respect to \(u\).

$$F(x,y)=2(1-e^{-y})\left[\frac{e^{-2u}}{-2}\right]_0^x=-(1-e^{-y})\left[e^{-2x}-1\right]=(1-e^{-y})(1-e^{-2x})$$

Hence,

$$F(x,y)=\begin{cases}(1-e^{-2x})(1-e^{-y}), & x\geq0,\;y\geq0\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Relationship between the joint PDF and joint CDF

  • PDF to CDF: integrate, \(F(x,y)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{y}f(u,v)\,dv\,du\).
  • CDF to PDF: differentiate with respect to both variables, $$f(x,y)=\frac{\partial^2F(x,y)}{\partial x\,\partial y}$$

Example 2 — joint PDF and marginal densities from a CDF

The joint distribution function of \(X\) and \(Y\) is

$$F(x,y)=\begin{cases}(1-e^{-x})(1-e^{-y}), & x\gt0,\;y\gt0\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Find (i) the joint PDF of \(X\) and \(Y\), and (ii) the marginal densities of \(X\) and \(Y\).

(i) Find the joint PDF

Use \(f(x,y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial y}F(x,y)\right]\). Differentiating with respect to \(y\), since \(1-e^{-x}\) is constant with respect to \(y\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(1-e^{-y})=e^{-y}\),

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left[(1-e^{-x})(1-e^{-y})\right]=(1-e^{-x})e^{-y}$$

Differentiating with respect to \(x\), since \(e^{-y}\) is constant with respect to \(x\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(1-e^{-x})=e^{-x}\),

$$f(x,y)=e^{-y}e^{-x}=e^{-(x+y)}$$

Thus, the joint PDF is \(f(x,y)=e^{-(x+y)}\) for \(x\gt0,\;y\gt0\), and 0 otherwise.

(ii) Marginal densities

The marginal density of one random variable is obtained by integrating the joint PDF over all possible values of the other:

$$f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy,\qquad f_Y(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx$$

Since the joint PDF is nonzero only for \(x\gt0,\;y\gt0\), the limits become 0 to \(\infty\). For \(X\), using \(e^{-(x+y)}=e^{-x}e^{-y}\),

$$f_X(x)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(x+y)}\,dy=e^{-x}\int_0^\infty e^{-y}\,dy=e^{-x}\left[-e^{-y}\right]_0^\infty=e^{-x}\cdot1=e^{-x}$$

so \(f_X(x)=e^{-x}\) for \(x\gt0\), and 0 otherwise. Similarly, for \(Y\),

$$f_Y(y)=\int_0^\infty e^{-(x+y)}\,dx=e^{-y}\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\,dx=e^{-y}\cdot1=e^{-y}$$

so \(f_Y(y)=e^{-y}\) for \(y\gt0\), and 0 otherwise.

Here \(f(x,y)=f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)\), so \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent. The same is true in Example 1, where \(f(x,y)=2e^{-2x}\cdot e^{-y}\).

Second problem: \(f(x,y)=e^{-(x+y)}\), with \(f_X(x)=e^{-x}\) for \(x\gt0\) and \(f_Y(y)=e^{-y}\) for \(y\gt0\).

Joint probability distribution: two unfair coins

A joint probability distribution gives the probabilities associated with combinations of outcomes of two or more random variables: the probability that several random variables take particular values, or fall within particular ranges, at the same time.

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be the outcomes of tossing two different unfair coins. For each coin, \(P(\text{Heads})=\frac23\) and \(P(\text{Tails})=\frac13\). The possible joint outcomes are (\(A=H,B=H\)), (\(A=H,B=T\)), (\(A=T,B=H\)) and (\(A=T,B=T\)). Since the two tosses are independent, the joint probabilities are obtained by multiplying the individual probabilities:

$$P(A=H,B=H)=\frac23\times\frac23=\frac49,\qquad P(A=T,B=H)=\frac13\times\frac23=\frac29$$ $$P(A=H,B=T)=\frac23\times\frac13=\frac29,\qquad P(A=T,B=T)=\frac13\times\frac13=\frac19$$

The joint probability distribution can therefore be represented as:

\(A=H\)\(A=T\)
\(B=H\)\(4/9\)\(2/9\)
\(B=T\)\(2/9\)\(1/9\)

Notice that \(\frac49+\frac29+\frac29+\frac19=1\), as required for a probability distribution.

Marginal distribution and the sum rule

Suppose we have the probability distribution of a set of random variables. The distribution of only a subset of those variables is called a marginal probability distribution. From the joint distribution of \(A\) and \(B\) we can obtain \(P(A)\) or \(P(B)\) by adding the appropriate joint probabilities.

Sum rule (discrete). $$P(X=x)=\sum_y P(X=x,Y=y)$$ To obtain the probability of \(X=x\), add the joint probabilities over all possible values of \(Y\).

For the coin example, including the marginal probabilities gives:

\(A=H\)\(A=T\)\(P(B)\)
\(B=H\)\(4/9\)\(2/9\)\(2/3\)
\(B=T\)\(2/9\)\(1/9\)\(1/3\)
\(P(A)\)\(2/3\)\(1/3\)

To find \(P(A=H)\), add the joint probabilities in the \(A=H\) column:

$$P(A=H)=P(A=H,B=H)+P(A=H,B=T)=\frac49+\frac29=\frac69=\frac23$$ $$P(A=T)=P(A=T,B=H)+P(A=T,B=T)=\frac29+\frac19=\frac39=\frac13$$

Marginal probability for a continuous random variable

In the continuous case, summation is replaced by integration:

$$p(x)=\int p(x,y)\,dy$$

Thus, the marginal density of \(X\) is obtained by integrating the joint density over all possible values of \(Y\).

Marginal PDFs of continuous random variables

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be continuous random variables with joint PDF \(f(x,y)\). The individual distributions of \(X\) and \(Y\), obtained from the joint PDF, are called their marginal probability density functions.

Marginal PDFs. Integrate the joint PDF over all values of the other variable: $$f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy\qquad\qquad f_Y(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx$$ In words: the marginal PDF of \(X\) is the joint PDF integrated over \(y\), and the marginal PDF of \(Y\) is the joint PDF integrated over \(x\).

Independence of continuous random variables

Independence. Two continuous random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if their joint PDF is the product of their marginal PDFs: $$f(x,y)=f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)$$ If this equality holds throughout the support of the variables, \(X\) and \(Y\) are statistically independent.

Example 1 — marginals and independence for f(x, y) = xy/96

The joint PDF is

$$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{xy}{96}, & 0\lt x\lt4,\;1\lt y\lt5\\[5pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Find (i) the marginal distributions of \(X\) and \(Y\), and (ii) whether \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.

(i) Marginal PDF of X

The joint PDF is nonzero only for \(1\lt y\lt5\), so for \(0\lt x\lt4\), taking the terms independent of \(y\) outside the integral,

$$f_X(x)=\int_1^5\frac{xy}{96}\,dy=\frac{x}{96}\int_1^5y\,dy=\frac{x}{96}\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_1^5=\frac{x}{192}[25-1]=\frac{24x}{192}=\frac x8$$

Outside this interval the joint PDF is zero. Hence \(f_X(x)=\frac x8\) for \(0\lt x\lt4\), and 0 otherwise.

Marginal PDF of Y

The joint PDF is nonzero only for \(0\lt x\lt4\), so for \(1\lt y\lt5\),

$$f_Y(y)=\int_0^4\frac{xy}{96}\,dx=\frac{y}{96}\int_0^4x\,dx=\frac{y}{96}\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^4=\frac{y}{192}[16-0]=\frac{16y}{192}=\frac y{12}$$

Hence \(f_Y(y)=\frac y{12}\) for \(1\lt y\lt5\), and 0 otherwise.

(ii) Check whether X and Y are independent

Within the support \(0\lt x\lt4\), \(1\lt y\lt5\), we have \(f_X(x)=\frac x8\) and \(f_Y(y)=\frac y{12}\). Therefore,

$$f_X(x)f_Y(y)=\frac x8\times\frac y{12}=\frac{xy}{96}=f(x,y)$$

Outside the given intervals, one of the marginals is 0 and the joint PDF is also 0. Therefore, \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent random variables.

Example 2 — marginals and independence for f(x, y) = ke−(2x+y)

The joint PDF is

$$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}ke^{-(2x+y)}, & x\geq0,\;y\geq0\\[5pt] 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Find (a) \(k\), (b) the marginal PDFs, and (c) whether \(X\) and \(Y\) are statistically independent.

(a) Find k

The total probability must be 1. Since \(e^{-(2x+y)}=e^{-2x}e^{-y}\),

$$\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty ke^{-(2x+y)}\,dy\,dx=k\int_0^\infty e^{-2x}\left(\int_0^\infty e^{-y}\,dy\right)dx=1$$

Now \(\int_0^\infty e^{-y}\,dy=\left[\frac{e^{-y}}{-1}\right]_0^\infty=-(0-1)=1\), so \(k\int_0^\infty e^{-2x}\,dx=1\), that is,

$$k\left[\frac{e^{-2x}}{-2}\right]_0^\infty=-\frac k2[0-1]=\frac k2=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad k=2$$

Thus, the joint PDF is \(f(x,y)=2e^{-(2x+y)}\) for \(x\geq0,\;y\geq0\), and 0 otherwise.

(b) Marginal PDF of X

Since \(y\geq0\), and \(2e^{-2x}\) does not depend on \(y\),

$$f_X(x)=\int_0^\infty2e^{-2x}e^{-y}\,dy=2e^{-2x}\int_0^\infty e^{-y}\,dy=2e^{-2x}$$

for \(x\geq0\), and \(f_X(x)=0\) for \(x\lt0\).

Marginal PDF of Y

Since \(x\geq0\), and \(e^{-y}\) does not depend on \(x\),

$$f_Y(y)=\int_0^\infty2e^{-2x}e^{-y}\,dx=2e^{-y}\left[\frac{e^{-2x}}{-2}\right]_0^\infty=-e^{-y}[0-1]=e^{-y}$$

for \(y\geq0\), and \(f_Y(y)=0\) for \(y\lt0\).

(c) Check statistical independence

For \(x\geq0\), \(y\geq0\),

$$f_X(x)f_Y(y)=2e^{-2x}e^{-y}=2e^{-(2x+y)}=f(x,y)$$

For \(x\lt0\) or \(y\lt0\), the corresponding marginal PDF is zero and the joint PDF is also zero. Hence \(X\) and \(Y\) are statistically independent.

Marginals and independence: quick summary

Summary. For continuous \(X\) and \(Y\) with joint PDF \(f(x,y)\): $$f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy,\qquad f_Y(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx,\qquad\text{independent}\iff f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)$$ Example 1: \(f_X(x)=\frac x8\) on \(0\lt x\lt4\) and \(f_Y(y)=\frac y{12}\) on \(1\lt y\lt5\); since \(\frac x8\cdot\frac y{12}=\frac{xy}{96}=f(x,y)\), \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent. Conditional distributions

Conditional distribution. $$p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=\frac{p(x,y)}{p_Y(y)}\qquad f_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=\frac{f(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}$$ defined where the denominator is positive. For each fixed \(y\) this is itself a valid distribution over \(x\).

Rearranged, \(p(x,y)=p_Y(y)\,p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\); repeated, this is the chain rule \(p(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i\mid x_1,\dots,x_{i-1})\).

Conditional expectation: \(E[X\mid Y=y]=\sum_x x\,p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)\). The law of total expectation states \(E[X]=E\big[E[X\mid Y]\big]\).

Part (d) of the contingency-table example in section 13 is a conditional probability: \(P(\text{Brown}\mid\text{Female})=\frac{29}{57}\approx0.509\).

Conditional probability from a probability table

Sometimes knowing that one event has already occurred changes the probability of another event. This leads to conditional probability.

Conditional probability. The probability that a second event \(B\) occurs given that we know the first event \(A\) has already occurred, read "the probability of B given A". Here \(A\) and \(B\) come from two different variables or experiments. $$P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}$$

Here \(P(A\cap B)\) is the joint probability of \(A\) and \(B\), and \(P(A)\) is the marginal probability of the event that has already occurred. To calculate it:

  • Step 1: find the joint probability \(P(A\cap B)\).
  • Step 2: find the marginal probability \(P(A)\) of the event that has already occurred.
  • Step 3: divide the joint probability by the marginal probability.

Example — cheating on a college exam

College students were asked whether they had ever cheated on an exam, and the results were broken down by gender.

Given that a person has cheated, what is the probability that the person is male?

Step 1: identify the conditional probability

We already know the selected person has cheated and want the probability that the person is male, so we need \(P(\text{Male}\mid\text{Cheated})\).

Step 2: find the joint probability

From the table, \(P(\text{Male}\cap\text{Cheated})=0.32\).

Step 3: find the marginal probability of cheating

From the bottom of the Cheated: Yes column, \(P(\text{Cheated})=0.60\).

Conditional probability for random variables

Conditional probability gives the probability that one outcome occurs given that another has already occurred. The probability that \(Y=y\) given that \(X=x\) is written \(P(Y=y\mid X=x)\):

$$P(Y=y\mid X=x)=\frac{P(Y=y,X=x)}{P(X=x)},\qquad\text{i.e.}\quad\text{Conditional}=\frac{\text{Joint}}{\text{Marginal}}$$

Example — the unfair coins

Given that coin \(B\) shows Tails, what is the probability that coin \(A\) shows Heads? From the table, \(P(A=H,B=T)=\frac29\) and \(P(B=T)=\frac13\). Therefore,

$$P(A=H\mid B=T)=\frac{P(A=H,B=T)}{P(B=T)}=\frac{2/9}{1/3}=\frac29\times3=\frac23$$

Chain rule of probability

Using conditional probabilities, we can express a joint probability of several variables in terms of conditional ones. Given \(P(a,b,c)\), find \(P(c)\). Start with \(P(a\mid b,c)=\frac{P(a,b,c)}{P(b,c)}\), which rearranges to \(P(a,b,c)=P(a\mid b,c)P(b,c)\). Now \(P(b\mid c)=\frac{P(b,c)}{P(c)}\), so \(P(b,c)=P(b\mid c)P(c)\). Substituting,

$$P(a,b,c)=P(a\mid b,c)\,P(b\mid c)\,P(c)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad P(c)=\frac{P(a,b,c)}{P(a\mid b,c)\,P(b\mid c)}$$

General chain rule

For random variables \(x^{(1)},x^{(2)},\ldots,x^{(n)}\), the joint probability decomposes into a product of conditional probabilities:

$$P(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})=P(x^{(1)})\prod_{i=2}^{n}P\left(x^{(i)}\mid x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(i-1)}\right)$$

Independence and correlation

Independence. \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if \(p(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y)\) for all \(x,y\) (densities likewise). Equivalently, \(p_{X\mid Y}(x\mid y)=p_X(x)\): knowing \(Y\) tells you nothing about \(X\).

In the table, \(P(\text{Brown})P(\text{Female})=\frac{60}{111}\cdot\frac{57}{111}\approx 0.278\), but \(P(\text{Brown and Female})\approx 0.261\). These differ, so eye colour and gender are not independent in this sample.

Covariance and correlation. $$\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y],\qquad \rho_{XY}=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}\in[-1,1]$$
  • \(\rho=+1\) or \(-1\) means a perfect linear relationship; \(\rho=0\) means uncorrelated.
  • \(\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}(X)+\mathrm{Var}(Y)+2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)\).
  • Independent \(\Rightarrow\) uncorrelated, but not the reverse. Take \(X\) uniform on \(\{-1,0,1\}\) and \(Y=X^2\): then \(E[XY]=E[X^3]=0=E[X]E[Y]\), so \(\mathrm{Cov}=0\), yet \(Y\) is completely determined by \(X\).

Conditional independence: \(X\perp Y\mid Z\) if \(p(x,y\mid z)=p(x\mid z)\,p(y\mid z)\).

Independence of random variables

Two events or random variables are independent if the outcome of one does not affect the probability of the outcome of the other. For example, consider two different people living in two different cities and whether each gets home for dinner on time. Let \(X\) be the outcome for the first person and \(Y\) the outcome for the second. If the variables are independent, their joint probability can be written as

$$P(x,y)=P(x)\,P(y),\qquad\text{written }X\perp Y$$

Conditional independence

Two variables may become independent when the value of a third variable is known. Again consider two people in two different cities trying to get home for dinner on time, and let \(Z\) represent whether there is snow in both cities. Then \(X\) and \(Y\) may be conditionally independent given \(Z\):

$$P(x,y\mid z)=P(x\mid z)\,P(y\mid z),\qquad\text{written }X\perp Y\mid Z$$

This means \(X\) and \(Y\) are conditionally independent given \(Z\).

Covariance

Covariance measures the joint variability of two random variables.

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=E\left[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])\right]$$
  • Negative covariance: one variable takes on relatively high values when the other takes on relatively low values, so the variables tend to move in opposite directions.
  • Positive covariance: both variables take on relatively high values at the same time, so they tend to move in the same direction.
  • High absolute covariance: both variables tend to take values far from their respective means together.

Covariance matrix

Suppose \(X=\begin{bmatrix}X_1\\ X_2\end{bmatrix}\) is a \(2\times1\) random vector. Its covariance matrix, \(\operatorname{Cov}(X)\), contains the variances and covariances of its components:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X)=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)\\[4pt] \operatorname{Cov}(X_2,X_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(X_2,X_2)\end{bmatrix}$$

Since \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_i)=\operatorname{Var}(X_i)\), the diagonal elements are the variances:

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X)=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{Var}(X_1) & \operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)\\[4pt] \operatorname{Cov}(X_2,X_1) & \operatorname{Var}(X_2)\end{bmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{Cov}(X)_{ij}=\operatorname{Cov}(X_i,X_j)$$

Chain rule\(P(a,b,c)=P(a\mid b,c)P(b\mid c)P(c)\)
Independence\(P(x,y)=P(x)P(y)\)
Conditional independence\(P(x,y\mid z)=P(x\mid z)P(y\mid z)\)
Expected value, discrete\(E[X]=\sum_iP(x_i)x_i\)
Expected value, continuous\(E(X)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x)x\,dx\)
Variance\(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E[(X-E[X])^2]\)

Where this appears in RL

  • Policy: \(\pi(a\mid s)=P(A_t=a\mid S_t=s)\) is a conditional PMF over actions.
  • Environment dynamics: \(p(s',r\mid s,a)\) is a conditional distribution that sums to 1 over \((s',r)\).
  • Markov property: the next state is conditionally independent of the past given the present state and action.
  • Value functions: \(v_\pi(s)=E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s]\), a conditional expectation of the return.
  • Sampling and variance: Monte Carlo estimates rely on linearity of expectation and on variance shrinking as samples are averaged.
